## What counts as domestic violence under the PWDVA (s.3)

The Act covers much more than physical beating. Domestic violence includes:

- **Physical abuse** — any act causing bodily pain, harm, or danger to life,
  limb, or health
- **Sexual abuse** — any conduct of a sexual nature that abuses, humiliates,
  degrades, or violates dignity
- **Verbal and emotional abuse** — insults, ridicule, humiliation (including
  for not having a child or a male child), repeated threats
- **Economic abuse** — depriving her of money or resources she is entitled
  to, taking away her salary or stridhan, stopping her from working,
  restricting access to the shared household

A woman does not need visible injuries for the law to protect her.

## Who is protected and against whom

The Act protects any woman in a domestic relationship — wives, live-in
partners, mothers, sisters, daughters, widows — against adult male
respondents and, in the case of a wife or partner, also against relatives
of the husband or partner (including female relatives).

## Orders a Magistrate can pass

A woman (or a Protection Officer or anyone on her behalf) can apply to the
Magistrate under **s.12**. The Magistrate can grant:

- **Protection orders (s.18)** — prohibiting the respondent from committing
  violence, entering her workplace, contacting her, alienating assets, or
  harming her relatives or anyone who helps her
- **Residence orders (s.19)** — she cannot be evicted from the shared
  household; the Magistrate can remove the respondent from the home, stop
  him from disposing of it, or direct him to provide alternate
  accommodation
- **Monetary relief (s.20)** — for loss of earnings, medical expenses,
  damage to property, and maintenance for her and her children
- **Custody orders (s.21)** — temporary custody of children to the woman
- **Compensation orders (s.22)** — for injuries including mental torture
  and emotional distress
- **Interim and ex parte orders (s.23)** — urgent orders can be passed
  quickly, before the respondent is even heard, if the application shows
  domestic violence is occurring or likely

## The right to residence (s.17)

Every woman in a domestic relationship has the right to reside in the
shared household, **whether or not she owns it or has any legal share in
it**. She cannot be evicted except by procedure established by law. Being
thrown out of the house is itself something the Magistrate can reverse.

## Key procedural facts

- Proceedings under the PWDVA are **civil in nature** — a woman can seek
  protection and residence orders without sending anyone to jail, which
  matters to many women who want the violence to stop but do not want to
  imprison the father of their children.
- **Breach of a protection order is a criminal offence (s.31)** —
  punishable with imprisonment; this gives orders teeth.
- A **Domestic Incident Report (DIR)** is recorded by a Protection Officer
  or service provider. It does not require an FIR.
- Criminal law also applies where she chooses: cruelty by a husband or his
  relatives is an offence under the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (s.85–86)**,
  which replaced IPC s.498A.
- She is entitled to **free legal aid** (s.9(1) and the Legal Services
  Authorities Act) and the court is expected to dispose of applications
  expeditiously.

## Safety planning (practical guidance)

A safety plan is a private, prepared set of steps for moments of danger.
Elements commonly recommended:

1. **A code word** with a trusted neighbour, friend, or family member that
   means "call 112 / come now."
2. **An exit bag** kept with a trusted person or hidden: copies of Aadhaar,
   PAN, marriage certificate, children's documents, some cash, spare keys,
   essential medicines.
3. **Memorise two numbers** — 181 and one trusted person — in case the
   phone is taken.
4. **Identify the safest room** — avoid kitchens and bathrooms (hard
   surfaces, weapons) when an argument escalates; stay near an exit.
5. **Plan for children** — teach them the code word and to go to a
   neighbour rather than intervene.
6. **Phone safety** — if the phone is monitored: use disappearing messages,
   delete call logs and chats relating to help-seeking, consider a second
   basic phone kept hidden, and do not save helpline numbers under obvious
   names.

## Documenting abuse safely (evidence guidance)

A dated pattern of evidence strengthens any future application under the
PWDVA. Safe documentation practices:

- **Write incidents down with dates** — what happened, where, who was
  present, injuries. Even brief notes matter; courts give weight to
  contemporaneous records.
- **Photograph injuries and damage** with timestamps, stored somewhere the
  abuser cannot reach (cloud account he doesn't know, or with a trusted
  person) — never only on the shared/home phone.
- **Get a medico-legal record (MLC)** at a government hospital after
  injuries — official, dated, and does not require an FIR.
- **Keep messages and call records** containing threats; back them up off
  the device.
- **Tell one person** — a friend, relative, doctor, or counsellor.
  Witnesses who can later say "she told me at the time" carry evidentiary
  and emotional weight.
- **Never confront the abuser with the evidence** — documentation is for
  her safety and her case, not for argument.

## Principles for anyone supporting a survivor

- Believe her; the first response she receives decides whether she asks
  again.
- The violence is never her fault, and "adjusting" is not a safety plan.
- Every decision — reporting, leaving, staying, seeking orders — is hers.
  Information empowers; pressure repeats the pattern of control.
- Leaving is statistically the most dangerous period; encourage planning
  with professionals (181, One Stop Centre, Protection Officer) rather than
  sudden unprepared departure when risk indicators are present.
